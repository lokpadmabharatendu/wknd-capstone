Trail Running the Alps

By Marta Keller

Trail Running the Alps

The first light spills over the Aiguilles as we lace up outside the refuge, breath fogging in the thin alpine air. Below us the valley is still asleep under a quilt of cloud; above, a ribbon of singletrack switchbacks toward a col we cannot yet see. There is no engine, no schedule, only the metronome of our own footfalls on the stone. This is trail running in the Alps — equal parts endurance, humility, and wonder.

The mountains do not care how fast you are — only that you keep moving.

verb

Running in the high mountains rearranges your sense of scale. A summit that looks an hour away hides three false tops and a scree field; a valley that seems a whole day distant arrives before lunch on a good descent. You learn to read the terrain like weather — to spend energy where the ground gives it back, and to walk, without shame, where the gradient demands it. The reward is a kind of moving meditation few other sports can offer.

Above the treeline

The Chamonix valley is a runner's cathedral. Endless miles of balcony trails traverse beneath the glaciers, linking hamlets, high pastures and stone refuges perched improbably on the ridgelines. The people you meet on the path — shepherds, alpinists, fellow runners from a dozen countries — share an easy camaraderie forged by altitude. And if you think the trail beneath your feet is beautiful, wait for the moment the clouds tear open and the whole Mont Blanc massif stands revealed, impossibly white against a hard blue sky.

Rhythm of the ridgeline

“Are you sure this is still the path?” Marta asks, half-laughing, as we pick our way across a boulder field toward the Col du Brevent. Somewhere ahead a cowbell clatters, and the smell of warm rock and wild thyme rises from the slope. We had set out to run a single loop, but one col led to another, and by afternoon we had strung together four passes and lost all track of time. Near the top a marmot whistles its alarm and vanishes; below, a paraglider peels off the ridge and banks silently over the valley. We stop, hands on knees, grinning at each other, and understand that the run was never really about the distance at all.

Refuge to refuge

There is a purity to running above the clouds that is hard to describe to anyone who has not felt it. For a few hours the noise of ordinary life falls away and the world narrows to breath, footfall, and the next switchback. We trade the last col for a long descent into Trient, quads burning, and arrive at a tiny refuge where the keeper pours us bowls of soup without a word. Other runners drift in as the light goes gold, boots muddy and faces sunburned, and we swap stories of the day’s trails in a half-dozen languages. As the first stars prick the sky over the massif, we understand that the summit was never the point — the point was the going, together, one ridgeline at a time.
